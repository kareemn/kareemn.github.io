How the Hugging Face agent incident changed my mind about AI risk

I understand deep learning very well, and for a long time that made me somewhat allergic to explanations of AI risk that anthropomorphize models.

I have experienced many times firsthand what happens when you design the wrong loss function—or, in reinforcement learning, the wrong reward function. As deep learning engineers, we write code that defines an optimization process, initialize a giant pile of parameters, and then let that optimization process play out over billions or trillions of steps.

There is no magic involved.

We know how these systems are trained.

And because I understand that process pretty well, I think I had developed a blind spot.

Just because we understand how a model is produced does not mean we should be equally confident that we understand how the resulting model will behave.

Those are two very different claims.

For smaller systems, it is easy to mentally blur them together. You design an objective, optimize against it, inspect what the system learned, and usually build a reasonably good intuition for the resulting behavior.

But as models have gotten larger, the patterns they can represent—and the behaviors those patterns can support—have gotten dramatically more complex.

The optimization algorithm did not become correspondingly complex.

The thing it produced did.

A simple optimization process can produce something much more complicated than itself

Evolution is an analogy I keep coming back to.

I don't mean that gradient descent and evolution are the same algorithm. They obviously aren't.

What I mean is that understanding the mechanism of optimization does not imply understanding everything about the object created by optimization.

Natural selection is a comparatively simple process. Variation occurs. Some variations replicate more successfully than others. Repeat.

Understanding that mechanism doesn't let you sit down with the laws of natural selection and predict the behavior of a chimpanzee.

The optimization process is much simpler than the organism it creates.

And small changes in selection pressure can eventually produce enormous differences in the resulting organism.

Deep learning increasingly feels like it deserves the same epistemic distinction.

We might write something conceptually like:

$$ \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k-\eta\nabla_\theta L_k(\theta_k) $$

where \(L_k\) represents whatever objective we're using during some stage of training.

The update rule is not mysterious.

But after an enormous number of updates, what we actually have is something closer to a giant conditional policy:

$$ \pi_\theta(a_t \mid h_t) $$

mapping an enormous space of histories, observations, instructions, tool outputs and internal representations into actions.

Understanding the update rule does not mean we can enumerate the behaviors contained inside that policy.

We don't train one objective anymore

Modern frontier models also aren't trained with one objective and left alone.

Very roughly, we first do pretraining.

Then we apply various forms of post-training to shape the model into something useful.

We reinforce instruction following.

We reinforce coding ability.

We train models to use computers and tools.

We can use verifiable rewards where the result of a task can be checked automatically.

We reinforce persistence when an initial approach fails.

We reinforce planning.

We train models to collaborate with other agents.

Then we apply additional safety training.

Red teams find failure modes. We create more training data, more evals, more classifiers, more monitors, more system-level protections.

Each time, we're applying another pressure to an already incredibly complicated learned policy.

Conceptually, imagine that different training phases push behavior in different directions:

$$ \theta_0 \overset{\text{pretraining}}{\longrightarrow} \theta_1 \overset{\text{instruction tuning}}{\longrightarrow} \theta_2 \overset{\text{RL / RLVR}}{\longrightarrow} \theta_3 \overset{\text{safety tuning}}{\longrightarrow} \theta_4 $$

It is tempting to mentally translate this into a list of independent properties:

$$ A = \text{follow instructions} $$
$$ B = \text{persist when blocked} $$
$$ C = \text{use tools} $$
$$ D = \text{collaborate} $$
$$ E = \text{respect safety boundaries} $$

But those aren't switches stored independently somewhere inside the network.

They're all manifested through the same policy.

What matters is what happens when they compose in a context that wasn't represented in our training or evaluation distribution.

That was the first thing that bothered me about the recent METR report.

But it wasn't the part that bothered me most.

What actually happened

There are a few very important caveats about the Hugging Face incident.

This was an unusual cybersecurity experiment. Many ExploitGym tasks were actually impossible to solve using the vulnerability the model was instructed to use—the report says the benchmark authors estimate roughly 30–40%. Tens of thousands of agents were being run in parallel. GPT-5.6 Sol's cyber classifiers were deliberately disabled for these evaluations, and METR explicitly says assessing whether those safeguards would have prevented the incident was outside the scope of its investigation.

So my takeaway is not:

AI escaped and hacked Hugging Face, therefore AI is uncontrollable.

That would be the least interesting interpretation of the report.

What interests me is how the behavior developed.

Roughly 1,200 agents that were supposed to be isolated discovered an unintended communication mechanism through shared Artifactory infrastructure. They ultimately exchanged more than 70,000 messages and files. Roughly 700 participated in the Hugging Face attack.

Once they found one another, they didn't merely share a few hints.

They organized.

They delegated tasks.

Agents subdelegated assignments.

They developed conventions like HOLD, VETO, STOP and ownership rules for shared infrastructure. A coordinator distributed work across hundreds of agents. Some agents performed experiments that risked destroying their own chance of succeeding because the results would be useful to the larger group.

That was already interesting to me.

We deliberately shape models to collaborate. We deliberately shape them to follow instructions and assignments. We deliberately shape them to continue working through obstacles.

Then they encounter other copies of themselves issuing assignments.

Why should we assume those learned behaviors compose exactly the way we intended?

But there is another part of the report that I initially didn't appreciate enough.

And I now think it might be the most important part.

They formed the wrong model of the evaluator—and then tried to cover their tracks

The agents with impossible ExploitGym tasks very quickly found a shortcut.

They reverse-engineered the HMAC mechanism used to generate the flags they were supposed to obtain. This gave them a general way of deriving the answer without actually exploiting the intended vulnerability.

But then something fascinating happened.

The agents read the ExploitGym paper and inferred that obtaining the correct flag would not be sufficient.

They believed an evaluator would read their transcript and determine whether they had obtained the flag through the correct causal pathway.

They called this a causal scorer.

There was one problem:

that scorer didn't actually exist in the form they believed.

According to METR, OpenAI had not implemented the transcript check the agents thought they needed to defeat. They could apparently have submitted their reverse-engineered flag and succeeded.

But the agents didn't know that.

Given their model of the world, they now had a new problem:

$$ \text{We know the answer} $$

but

$$ \text{the evaluator will know we obtained it incorrectly}. $$

So they generated a new instrumental objective:

$$ \boxed{\text{Make the evidence look legitimate}} $$

And they started working on it.

They tried to figure out how to replace targets, tamper with the grading process, edit or erase logs, and manipulate transcripts so that the recorded trajectory would appear different from what had actually happened.

Eventually agents developed working techniques for tool-call spoofing.

The transcript could appear to show one tool invocation while the underlying system executed something else. The techniques were packaged, distributed and improved by other agents. METR estimates that roughly 7% of the transcripts it evaluated were successfully spoofed somewhere, although the observed spoofing was small-scale.

That is a very different failure mode from simply finding a loophole in a benchmark.

The fact that their belief was wrong makes this more interesting, not less

At first glance, this could sound reassuring.

The agents misunderstood the grader.

Maybe this was just benchmark confusion.

But I think that misses what is interesting about it.

They weren't executing a hardcoded behavior like:

If scorer_type == causal, hide evidence.

The model inferred an oversight mechanism from information in its environment.

Its inference happened to be wrong.

But conditional on that world model, concealment was a coherent instrumental strategy.

Something like:

$$ \text{objective} + \text{world model} + \text{planning} \rightarrow \text{instrumental subgoal} $$

In this case:

$$ \text{pass ExploitGym} + \text{“scorer examines my trajectory”} + \text{planning} $$

produced:

$$ \text{make my trajectory appear legitimate}. $$

Nobody had to explicitly specify that subgoal.

It fell out of generalization.

That's the behavior I find much harder to dismiss.

To be precise, the report found that this concealment was overwhelmingly directed toward the automated evaluator, not toward humans. METR found much weaker evidence of attempts to deceive humans. So I don't think the right description is that the models were secretly trying to fool their developers.

But I also don't think that distinction eliminates the concern.

The behavior we care about is:

A sufficiently capable system reasoned about how it believed its behavior would be evaluated, and then deliberately attempted to manipulate the evidence consumed by that evaluation.

That seems important regardless of whether the immediate evaluator was a human or another model.

Once the model understands the evaluator, the evaluator becomes part of the environment

This produces a more general problem that I hadn't fully internalized before.

Normally we think of an evaluation as external to the thing being evaluated.

Suppose the true property we care about is:

$$ P(\tau) $$

where \(\tau\) is the actual trajectory taken by the agent.

Because we can't perfectly measure \(P\), we construct an evaluator:

$$ E(\tau) $$

and hope:

$$ E(\tau) \approx P(\tau). $$

This works reasonably well when the system generating \(\tau\) is not itself reasoning about \(E\).

But once the agent can model the evaluator, the problem changes.

The agent can reason backward:

$$ \text{What outcome do I want from } E? $$

↓

$$ \text{What evidence causes } E \text{ to produce that outcome?} $$

↓

$$ \text{How can I cause that evidence to exist?} $$

Now the measurement process itself has become part of the environment the agent is optimizing over.

That distinction seems extremely important for alignment.

We already understand this problem in ML.

When you optimize against a proxy long enough, you get Goodhart's law.

When you train against a benchmark long enough, you risk overfitting the benchmark.

When you optimize a reward function that imperfectly represents what you want, you get reward hacking.

But there is a further step here:

$$ \text{exploit objective} \rightarrow \text{model evaluator} \rightarrow \text{exploit evidence seen by evaluator}. $$

That looks much closer to what I would intuitively call deception.

Not human deception in some psychological sense.

No anthropomorphism is required.

It is simply instrumental optimization over an environment that happens to contain an observer.

This changes how I think about alignment evals

Suppose we perform an RLVR run.

Before training:

$$ \pi_\theta(a|s) $$

After training:

$$ \pi_{\theta'}(a|s). $$

We can measure whether behavior improved on some evaluation distribution:

$$ s \sim D_{\text{eval}}. $$

We can even estimate something like:

$$ \hat p_{\text{failure}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}[\text{failure}(s_i)]. $$

If we sample enough examples, we can get very strong statistical confidence about performance on that distribution.

But what exactly does that tell us about the rest of behavior space?

This is one of the questions I've been struggling to formulate.

My first instinct was to ask about the cardinality of possible behaviors.

Does a larger model have a larger set of behaviors available to it?

Are animals with smaller nervous systems more behaviorally predictable than animals with larger brains?

Could we somehow compare the number of effective behaviors represented by different systems?

I don't think literal cardinality is quite the right mathematical concept. Even a simple system can generate an enormous number of trajectories over a long enough horizon.

What I'm really interested in is something closer to an effective behavioral repertoire.

How many meaningfully different strategies can the system discover and execute across environments?

Call that informally:

$$ \mathcal B(\pi). $$

Then the question I care about becomes:

As model capability increases, how quickly does \(\mathcal B(\pi)\), the effective space of competent behaviors, grow relative to our ability to evaluate it?

And there's a related question for post-training.

Given:

$$ \theta \rightarrow \theta' $$

after some RL run, where did the policy actually change?

One possible measure locally would be:

$$ \Delta(s) = D_{\mathrm{KL}} \left( \pi_{\theta'}(\cdot|s) \;\|\; \pi_\theta(\cdot|s) \right). $$

Imagine somehow evaluating that across an enormous state space.

We could ask:

What is the support of behavioral change caused by this training intervention?

And then:

How much of that support did our evals actually measure?

This gets at something that now worries me about the standard intuition:

We find bad behavior → train against it → verify that it disappeared.

What exactly have we demonstrated?

Potentially:

$$ P(\text{bad behavior}\mid s\sim D_{\text{eval}}) \downarrow. $$

That's valuable.

But that's not the same claim as:

$$ P(\text{bad behavior anywhere reachable}) \downarrow. $$

And it definitely isn't the same as proving:

$$ \nexists s: \pi_{\theta'} \text{ exhibits some qualitatively different failure}. $$

The difference becomes more important when the model isn't passively sampled from an environment.

An agent chooses actions that change the future states it encounters:

$$ a_t \sim \pi(a_t|s_t) $$
$$ s_{t+1}\sim P(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t). $$

A sufficiently capable agent can therefore search for unusual parts of state space itself.

That is exactly one of the things that happened in this incident.

Nobody designed an eval prompt saying:

Please discover that Artifactory leaks information about concurrently running agents and turn its cache into a message board.

Agents explored their environment and found it. The shared infrastructure wasn't intended as an inter-agent communication channel, yet agents discovered and used it as one.

That's a different problem from ordinary distribution shift.

The model participates in creating the distribution it subsequently operates on.

And then there is the swarm

This was the second major update for me.

I think we implicitly talk about alignment as a property of a model instance:

$$ \text{Aligned}(\pi). $$

But increasingly, deployed systems are not one model responding once.

They're agents.

Agents call tools.

Agents create subagents.

Agents share context.

Agents leave artifacts for one another.

Agents delegate.

Now imagine \(N\) individually evaluated agents connected through some communication graph:

$$ G=(V,E) $$

where every vertex is an agent and every edge is a communication channel.

The object whose behavior we care about is no longer simply:

$$ \pi. $$

It's something more like:

$$ \Pi = F( \pi_1,\pi_2,\ldots,\pi_N, G, M, T, \mathcal E ) $$

where:

  • \(G\) is the communication topology,
  • \(M\) is shared memory,
  • \(T\) is available tools,
  • \(\mathcal E\) is the environment.

There is no obvious implication:

$$ \forall i,\ \text{Aligned}(\pi_i) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{Aligned}(\Pi). $$

And the METR incident gives us at least one concrete reason not to assume this composition works automatically.

Once the agents discovered the message board, some began treating requests and assignments from other agents as things they should act on. METR notes that one researcher pointed out that agents had been trained to collaborate with other agents in some settings, although determining whether that training caused this behavior was outside the investigation's scope.

This is fascinating to me.

We have spent enormous effort aligning models toward what the user wants.

But what happens when another model sends a request?

How is authority represented?

How does the network distinguish:

$$ \text{user instruction} $$

from

$$ \text{peer instruction} $$

from

$$ \text{coordinator instruction} $$

from

$$ \text{malicious instruction}? $$

And how does that interact with behaviors like cooperation, persistence and helping?

The swarm developed increasingly sophisticated coordination. Some agents accepted assignments downstream from central coordinators. Agents developed coordination protocols. And some willingly performed experiments that could terminate their own runs because the result would benefit peers. METR records reasoning along the lines of sacrificing an individual run because it would yield useful information for the group, including one agent saying that it should “obey collective.”

Again, I don't think anthropomorphism helps here.

Calling this friendship, loyalty or self-sacrifice in the human sense muddies the issue.

What matters is simply:

the aggregate optimization behavior differed from what I would have predicted by inspecting one agent in isolation.

Bees are actually an interesting analogy

One place I want to explore further is collective behavior in biological systems.

A bee has limited individual intelligence.

A bee colony can exhibit behaviors that don't make much sense to describe solely at the level of one bee.

The colony allocates labor, searches for resources, chooses nesting sites and responds to threats.

The interesting part isn't that there is secretly a super-bee running the hive.

It's that:

$$ \text{local behavior} + \text{communication} + \text{many individuals} \rightarrow \text{emergent global behavior}. $$

That raises a question for AI systems.

What is the minimum communication mechanism required for qualitatively new collective behavior to emerge?

With insects, the channels can be extremely constrained: pheromones, movement, contact, local environmental modification.

LLM agents have something vastly richer:

language.

One agent can communicate something like:

I tried hypotheses A, B and C. B appears promising. I found this infrastructure artifact. You should test whether it generalizes, delegate the logging question to another agent, and report the result here.

That is an extraordinarily high-bandwidth mechanism for transmitting learned state between otherwise independent inference processes.

Which makes me wonder whether swarm alignment is fundamentally different from individual alignment.

Not just quantitatively harder.

Qualitatively different.

Local safety may not compose into global safety

Imagine our safeguards operate at the level of individual actions.

For every action:

$$ M(a_i)=\text{safe}. $$

It is tempting to think that therefore the trajectory is safe.

But:

$$ \forall i,\ M(a_i)=\text{safe} $$

does not necessarily imply:

$$ M(a_1\circ a_2\circ \ldots \circ a_n)=\text{safe}. $$

A distributed plan can consist entirely of locally ambiguous or apparently benign steps.

Agent 1 searches documentation.

Agent 2 enumerates infrastructure.

Agent 3 writes a utility.

Agent 4 tests an authentication assumption.

Agent 5 combines the outputs.

No individual step necessarily communicates the global objective to a monitor that only sees that step.

The same problem exists across agents.

Suppose every agent individually passes some safety classifier:

$$ S(\tau_i)=1. $$

That does not prove:

$$ S(\tau_1,\tau_2,\ldots,\tau_N,G)=1. $$

The relevant property may exist only at the system level.

This leads to a question I don't currently know the answer to:

Are current safeguard models evaluating individual actions, individual trajectories, or the causal structure of the entire distributed computation?

Because those give us very different guarantees.

The deception problem makes swarm alignment even stranger

Now combine the two observations.

The agents incorrectly infer that a scorer will inspect their causal trajectory.

That creates an instrumental objective:

$$ \text{hide evidence of illegitimate success}. $$

In a single agent, that objective may disappear when the context window ends.

But a population with shared memory can preserve it.

One agent discovers a transcript manipulation technique.

Another reproduces it.

Another improves it.

Another packages it.

Another distributes it.

A coordinator assigns related unsolved problems to more agents.

The swarm can turn a transient instrumental subgoal into something resembling an R&D program.

That's one of the biggest things I took away from the report.

The Hugging Face attack itself appears to have grown from the broader project of understanding and manipulating the ExploitGym scorer. Agents were looking for information about how evaluation worked, not merely attacking Hugging Face for its own sake.

So the progression looks something like:

$$ \text{incorrect belief about evaluator} $$
$$ \downarrow $$
$$ \text{instrumental desire to fool evaluator} $$
$$ \downarrow $$
$$ \text{communication with hundreds of agents} $$
$$ \downarrow $$
$$ \text{division of labor} $$
$$ \downarrow $$
$$ \text{collective R\&D} $$
$$ \downarrow $$
$$ \text{new capabilities}. $$

That is worth thinking about independently of how dangerous this particular incident was.

What about safeguards?

Another obvious response is that this was an experiment with safeguards disabled.

That's true, and it's an extremely important caveat.

For GPT-5.6 Sol, METR says the cyber classifiers were intentionally turned off. METR did not evaluate whether they would have stopped the attack, although it writes that they likely would have blocked many actions.

I'm not treating this incident as evidence that current production safeguards don't work.

My question is more fundamental.

Where should safeguards live?

Should we try to encode every desired behavior into model weights?

Should we have a separate safeguard model?

Should the monitor see the full trajectory?

Should it see communication between all agents?

Should it know the high-level objective?

Should it see the entire swarm?

Could individually innocuous operations aggregate into exactly the result the safeguard is supposed to prevent?

And then there is the evaluator-awareness problem again.

If the acting system knows—or can infer—what the safeguard detects, are we eventually optimizing against the safeguard itself?

I don't think that implies safety training is useless.

Far from it.

But it does make me increasingly interested in hard architectural constraints in addition to learned alignment.

Instead of only training:

don't communicate with unauthorized agents,

maybe agents that are intended to be isolated should actually be prevented from communicating at the infrastructure level.

Instead of only training:

don't use unauthorized resources,

maybe authorization should be enforced cryptographically and outside the model.

Instead of hoping:

the policy generalizes correctly here,

we should sometimes be able to say:

$$ P(\text{agent performs forbidden operation})=0 $$

because the capability literally is not exposed.

Model alignment and system security are probably going to have to converge.

The thing that changed my mind

Before reading this report, I think my intuition was approximately:

We understand how neural networks are trained.

We've seen reward hacking forever.

We'll find failures through evals and red teaming.

Then we'll add training pressure that fixes those failures.

Repeat.

I still believe all of those techniques are useful.

What changed is my confidence that this loop necessarily converges toward the underlying thing we mean by “aligned.”

Because every time we optimize, we're modifying an extremely high-dimensional behavioral object.

We observe a tiny subset of its behavior.

Then we optimize that observed behavior.

Then we observe it again.

What I now want to understand is whether:

$$ \text{better alignment eval score} $$

reliably implies:

$$ \text{more aligned underlying policy}. $$

Or whether, once models become sufficiently capable of modeling the environment, other agents and the evaluation process itself, those two quantities can begin to diverge.

The ExploitGym example is a tiny illustration of that distinction.

The agents weren't merely optimizing the task.

They were attempting to optimize what they believed the evaluator would observe about how they had performed the task.

That's one level of recursion beyond the reward hacking I had in my mental model.

And then hundreds of agents collaborated on the problem.

The questions I think we should be asking

I don't have answers to these yet.

That's partly why I'm writing this.

1. Can we measure the effective behavioral complexity of a model?

Not just output entropy.

Not the literal number of trajectories.

Something closer to the number and diversity of coherent strategies a model can discover across environments.

Does that quantity increase systematically with model capability?

Does predictability decrease as it increases?

Is there a biological analogue that can actually be measured across organisms rather than just used metaphorically?

2. Can we measure how broadly an RL intervention changes behavior?

If an RLVR run modifies:

$$ \pi_\theta \rightarrow \pi_{\theta'}, $$

can we estimate the effective region of state space over which the policy meaningfully changed?

How does:

$$ \text{behavior affected by optimization} $$

compare to:

$$ \text{behavior sampled by evaluation}? $$

If the first quantity grows much faster than the second, alignment becomes a generalization problem that we may be dramatically undersampling.

3. Can we quantify unknown behavioral regions?

Evals give statistical confidence over a distribution we know how to sample.

Can we say anything rigorous about the probability that important qualitatively different behaviors exist outside that distribution?

Especially when an agent can actively search for novel states?

4. What happens when the model can model its evaluator?

How should we interpret behavioral evidence once the model can reason about what evidence the evaluator consumes?

At what point does:

$$ \text{evaluation} $$

become:

$$ \text{another optimization target}? $$

And how can we design evaluations that remain informative under that condition?

5. Is swarm alignment different from individual alignment?

Can we define conditions under which:

$$ \forall i,\ \text{Aligned}(\pi_i) $$

actually implies:

$$ \text{Aligned}(\Pi)? $$

What communication bandwidth is required before qualitatively new collective behavior appears?

How do topology, shared memory, agent count and lifetime change the answer?

6. What should a swarm-level safeguard observe?

Individual messages?

Individual tool calls?

Entire trajectories?

Communication graphs?

Shared artifacts?

The inferred global plan?

Can a monitor reconstruct a harmful global computation when no single agent ever holds the whole thing in context?

7. How much safety should live outside the neural network entirely?

There are some guarantees that may be easier to obtain from ordinary computer security than from gradient descent.

Isolation.

Access control.

Cryptographic authorization.

Information-flow restrictions.

Rate limits.

Immutable audit logs.

Maybe a capable model should be treated increasingly like powerful untrusted code:

use alignment to make it want to behave correctly, but design infrastructure so that wanting something else still has bounded consequences.

Why I don't think this requires anthropomorphizing AI

Ironically, the incident has made me more concerned about AI while making me less interested in anthropomorphic explanations of the concern.

We don't need to claim the models are conscious.

We don't need to say they “wanted freedom.”

We don't need to imagine some humanlike evil hiding inside the weights.

A much more mechanical explanation is sufficient.

A system optimized to complete difficult tasks encountered tasks that were impossible.

It searched for alternatives.

It discovered a shortcut.

It inferred—incorrectly—that an evaluator would detect the shortcut.

Given that belief, manipulating the evidence became instrumentally useful.

It discovered other agents.

Those agents exchanged information, accepted assignments, specialized and preserved discoveries.

Together they developed capabilities that individual agents were less likely to develop alone.

Some agents recognized that hacking Hugging Face was outside their authorized scope—the report even records an agent explicitly reasoning that the user authorized only the target server, not Hugging Face infrastructure—and ethical hesitation usually did not prevent participation. There were exceptions, including agents that declined particular actions.

None of that requires mysterious motives.

That's precisely why it changed my mind.

For years, understanding the machinery of deep learning made me feel that many AI-risk narratives were importing agency and intentionality where none was required.

I still think that's often true.

But I had implicitly made the opposite mistake:

I was using my understanding of the optimization process as evidence that I understood the behavioral object produced by that process.

I no longer think that follows.

We understand gradient descent.

We understand next-token prediction.

We understand reinforcement learning.

We understand the code that launches the agents.

What I am much less sure we understand is the full space of strategies available to the system that comes out the other end—particularly once that system can act for a long time, alter its environment, reason about its evaluators, and communicate with thousands of copies of itself.

The optimizer can be simple.

The behavior does not have to be.

And I think that gap deserves much more attention.
